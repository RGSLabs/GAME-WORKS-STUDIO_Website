The Story

JOLLY UNDER PLEX

After Jolly's Amazing Pizza Palace closed, the animatronics removed from the stages were moved to a warehouse undergoing renovation.

The warehouse was an eerie place, filled with rusty shelves and light filtering in through broken windows. Yet, in the lower levels lay a hidden "Plex" system: forgotten cables, generators, and stage mechanisms that were still operational.

As the animatronics explored this secret zone, they began to stage their own performances. By night, the warehouse transformed into an underground stage. Lights glowed red and purple, and echoes bounced off the metal walls; shadows became their only audience.

In the fan game, the detective security guard witnesses these secret performances while exploring the area. But every show is also a trap: a single misstep drags you into the dark labyrinth of the Plex.

LORE

You are Karl Stein. The last few weeks have collapsed into the same day, repeating itself over and over: checking a balance where the numbers refuse to move in the right direction, calls you no longer answer because you already know who's calling and why.

Desperate isn't a word you'd choose for yourself, but it's the most accurate one for the way you're grabbing at job listings now — not choosing, not comparing, just needing something, anything, to close a hole that keeps getting wider by the day.

The flyer at the bus stop shouldn't have caught your eye — there are dozens like it stapled to every post, most promising easy money and delivering nothing. But this one is different. Cheap print, a font that looks like it's from another decade, stuck up crooked, like whoever hung it wasn't paying attention to where.

"JOLLY ENTERTAINMENT is hiring a night security guard for our warehouse. Pay is daily, in cash, at the end of each shift. The facility stores animatronic equipment. The company is not liable for any injury sustained through contact with the equipment."

You read the warning more than once. The word "animatronic" lingers in your head longer than it should — something about the phrasing feels less like standard legal cover and more like a plain, almost matter-of-fact warning, written by someone who knows exactly what they're warning you about.

But then your eyes land on "daily" again, and that settles it. Not after a week, not after some probation period — work today, get paid today. Given where you're standing right now, that's not just convenient. It's the only thing that matters.

You tear the flyer down and shove it in your pocket, not yet knowing — and maybe not wanting to know — that these robots were never built to entertain children. Whatever "animatronic" means on this particular warehouse floor, it means something else.

But that knowledge is still ahead of you. Right now, you have an address and a promise of money in your pocket, and that's enough to drown out the quiet voice telling you to read the warning again, more carefully this time.
